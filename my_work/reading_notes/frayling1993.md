# Research in Art and Design

## 1 · Why this paper

This paper is foundational to understanding how research is defined and legitimated in art and design disciplines. I encountered it as a core reference in discussions of practice-based research methodologies.

## 2 · Summary

Frayling addresses the contested meaning of "research" in art and design, arguing that stereotypes of the expressive artist, style-obsessed designer, and critical-rationalist scientist obscure the actual cognitive traditions in creative practice. The paper's main contribution is a three-part taxonomy: research into art and design (historical/theoretical), research through art and design (materials research, development work, action research), and research for art and design (where thinking is embodied in the artefact). This framework provides a practical vocabulary for distinguishing legitimate research activities from mere reference-gathering or personal expression. The problem is important because funding, academic status, and degree validation depend on how research is defined. The innovation is separating what research is studied (historical, theoretical) from how it is conducted (through studio practice) and what its output is (communicable knowledge vs. embodied understanding). Frayling advances the debate by moving beyond the "yes/no" question of whether art counts as research, offering instead a graded typology that preserves disciplinary difference while meeting academic standards.

## 3 · Critical thinking

The paper's most significant limitation is its reliance on historical examples (Stubbs, Constable, Leonardo) to argue for a cognitive tradition in art. These cases are now over a century old and involve observational science that has been superseded by electron microscopy and computational methods. Frayling concedes this point but does not address the implication: if contemporary artists cannot do novel anatomical research, what can they research that is genuinely new? The taxonomy's third category—research for art—remains underdeveloped; Frayling admits it "needs a great deal of further research" but does not specify assessment criteria. This leaves the category vulnerable to the very confusion the paper aims to resolve. The paper also assumes that academic legitimation is desirable, without questioning whether some art might thrive precisely because it resists research paradigms.

## 4 · Creative thinking

The taxonomy could be applied to adjacent fields such as architecture, music composition, or culinary arts, where similar debates about practice-based research occur. One could propose a parallel framework for "research through performance" or "research for choreography." The paper sparked the idea that the report or documentation accompanying studio work is the crucial differentiator—this suggests a method: require researchers to produce a "research diary" that makes tacit knowledge explicit, similar to ethnography's field notes. The distinction between "propositional" and "tacit" knowledge (borrowed from Collins) could be developed into a formal evaluation rubric for practice-based PhDs.

## 6 · Venue signal

This paper's acceptance by the Royal College of Art (a leading art and design institution) signals that the venue rewards conceptual clarity, historical grounding, and pragmatic frameworks that can be used for institutional decision-making. The paper is not experimental or data-driven—it is a rhetorical intervention designed to shape policy.

## 7 · Take-home

I learned that the "research vs. practice" debate is not new; it has been shaped by institutional accidents (1837 separation from universities) and Hollywood stereotypes. The key insight is that any research definition must specify what is being communicated (knowledge vs. artefact) and how it is documented. I can apply the three-part taxonomy when evaluating my own work: is it into, through, or for? This is a good writing sample to imitate for its structure: problem → stereotype critique → historical evidence → taxonomy → caveats. The tone is authoritative yet self-deprecating, which is rhetorically effective.
